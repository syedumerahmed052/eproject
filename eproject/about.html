Why we built this tribute — and what Pakistan's Nobel legacy means to the world.

Our Mission

Celebrating Pakistan's Contribution to Human Knowledge

This project was born out of a simple conviction: Pakistan's two Nobel Laureates deserve to be known by every child in the country, and by the world. Their stories are not just academic achievements — they are proof that intellect, courage, and compassion can shape history regardless of where you come from.

Dr. Abdus Salam's equations still underpin the Large Hadron Collider. Malala Yousafzai's voice still rings in UN halls. This site exists to keep those stories alive and accessible.

Meet the Laureates

Physics · 1979

Dr. Abdus Salam

Nobel 1979

Muhammad Abdus Salam (29 January 1926 – 21 November 1996) was a Pakistani theoretical physicist. He shared the 1979 Nobel Prize in Physics with Sheldon Glashow and Steven Weinberg for their contribution to the electroweak unification theory — the first time two of the four fundamental forces of nature had been shown to be different facets of the same phenomenon.

Born in Jhang, Punjab, he showed extraordinary ability from childhood, scoring the highest marks ever recorded for the matriculation exam at the University of the Punjab. He went on to Cambridge on a scholarship, returning to Pakistan briefly before settling in London and Trieste.

In 1964 he founded the International Centre for Theoretical Physics (now the Abdus Salam ICTP) in Trieste, Italy — an institution dedicated to fostering advanced study in the developing world. It has helped thousands of scientists from over 180 countries who would otherwise have had no access to frontier research.

"Scientific thought is the common heritage of mankind."
— Dr. Abdus Salam, Nobel Prize in Physics 1979

Peace · 2014

Malala Yousafzai

Nobel 2014

Malala Yousafzai (born 12 July 1997) is a Pakistani activist for female education and the youngest Nobel Prize laureate. Born in Mingora in the Swat District, she spoke out publicly against the Taliban's efforts to ban education for girls — writing under a pseudonym for BBC Urdu from age 11.

On 9 October 2012, a gunman boarded her school bus and shot her in the head. She survived after emergency treatment in Pakistan and the United Kingdom. Rather than silence her, the attack turned her into a global symbol of resistance against extremism.

In 2013 she co-authored I Am Malala with journalist Christina Lamb. She went on to found the Malala Fund — a charity dedicated to ensuring 12 years of free, quality education for every girl. At 17 she accepted the Nobel Peace Prize in Oslo, sharing it with Indian children's rights activist Kailash Satyarthi.
